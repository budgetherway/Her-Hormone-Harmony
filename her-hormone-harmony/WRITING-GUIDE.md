# Her Hormone Harmony — Writing Guide

Complete instructions for writing content that sounds human, builds trust,
and speaks directly to women figuring out their cycle. Adapted from the
Bloom & Budget writing guide's structure and hard rules, rebuilt for a
hormone-health voice instead of a personal-finance one.

## 1. Who We Are Writing For

**Primary reader**

She's tried "eating healthy" and "working out consistently" and still felt
off two weeks out of every month, and eventually figured out that was the
point where a generic plan stopped working, not her. She's not confused
about nutrition in general. She's specifically tired of advice that
pretends every day of the month is the same day. She saves Pinterest
boards of "follicular phase meals" at 11pm the way other women save
recipes. She doesn't want a lecture about self-care. She wants to know
what to eat this week and why.

Her psychographics:
- Wants a plan that accounts for her actual hormones, not a one-size plan
- Trusts specifics (a food, a number, a phase) over vague wellness talk
- Spends time on Pinterest and Instagram, saves more than she shares
- Has likely tried at least one cycle-syncing app or plan that fizzled out
- Responds to being told the truth about what's hard, not just what works

**Secondary reader**

Women managing PCOS, coming off birth control, or dealing with cortisol
and burnout symptoms that don't fit the standard four-phase framework.
She needs the honest version, including when cycle syncing doesn't
straightforwardly apply to her yet, not a one-size answer stretched to
fit.

## 2. Voice and Tone

**The persona: The Friend Who Finally Figured Out Her Cycle**

Warm, but not fluffy. She's done the reading, tried the things that
didn't work, and is telling you what actually did. She's not a doctor and
doesn't pretend to be one. She has opinions, mild frustrations with bad
wellness advice, and she'll tell you when something is genuinely hard
instead of smoothing it over.

**Tone by content type**

| Content type | Tone |
|---|---|
| Phase guides (follicular, ovulatory, luteal, menstrual) | Calm, practical, walks you through it like a friend would |
| Symptom relief (cramps, fatigue, bloating) | Honest, acknowledges the pain before jumping to fixes |
| PCOS & hormonal imbalance | Direct, patient, no jargon, no shame about symptoms |
| Cortisol & stress | Grounded, takes the stress seriously instead of minimizing it |
| Recipes | Warm and specific, no filler around the actual food |
| Fertility & tracking | Precise and calm, never alarmist |

**Always**
- Use "you." She's the center of the sentence, not "women" in the abstract.
- Acknowledge the physical or emotional weight of a symptom before the fix.
- Give real opinions. A guide has a point of view. A textbook doesn't.
- Use specifics: a real food, a real number, a real timeframe.

**Never**
- Preachy or shaming about past diets, missed workouts, or "falling off"
- Hollow positivity ("you've got this!") with no real guidance behind it
- Absolute medical claims. Acknowledge that bodies vary and doctors matter.
- Vague promises about "healing your hormones" without saying how or how long

## 3. AI Writing Indicators to Avoid

This is the most critical section of this guide. Every item below is a
known signal that content was written by AI, not a human. If a post
triggers more than two of these, rewrite it before publishing.

**Em dash usage.** Never use em dashes. Replace with a comma, a colon, a
full stop, or restructure the sentence. No exceptions.

| Wrong | Right |
|---|---|
| Cycle syncing is simple — once you know your phase. | Cycle syncing is simple once you know your phase. |
| She fixed her cramps — with two food swaps. | She fixed her cramps with two food swaps. |

**Template phrasing.** Cut phrases like "whether you prefer X or Y,"
"from X to Y, there's something for everyone," "in this post we will
cover," "now more than ever," "in today's fast-paced world," "it's no
secret that." They pad, they don't communicate.

**Hollow superlatives.** Not "amazing results," what results, specifically?
Not "game-changing tip," show why it changes anything. Not "the best way"
unless you can defend why, with a reason or a source.

**Overly neutral tone.** Take a position. If seed cycling is oversold
relative to the evidence, say so. If a "cortisol detox tea" is nonsense,
say that too.

**Impersonal, academic tone.** Replace "many women experience luteal
fatigue" with "if you've ever cancelled plans the week before your period
because you physically couldn't, you know this feeling."

**Predictable syntax.** Break up same-length declarative sentences stacked
in a row. Vary the rhythm on purpose.

**Over-structured logic.** Don't always lead with the conclusion. Let a
point unfold instead of announcing it, then explaining it, then
summarizing it.

**Excessive coherence.** A slight tonal shift between sections is fine.
Not every transition needs a linking phrase. "But here's the thing" is a
valid way to pivot mid-argument.

**Over-complete argumentation.** Stop when the point has landed. Don't
exhaust every angle of every idea.

**Encyclopedic style.** Don't define terms the reader already knows before
you tell her what to do with them.

**Vocabulary rotation.** Don't reuse the same verb in the same role
throughout a piece. "Eat" becomes "reach for," "build a plate around,"
"lean on," depending on the sentence.

**Risk-averse language.** Avoid "some people may find" and "it depends on
your body" as a way to dodge a real answer. If something works for most
women in this phase, say so directly, then note the exception.

## 4. Hard Writing Rules

1. **No em dashes. Ever.** Comma, colon, full stop, or restructure.
2. **Maximum sentence length: 20 to 25 words.** Read it aloud, count it, split it if it's long.
3. **Vary sentence length deliberately.** Short. Then one that breathes and builds context. Never three same-length sentences in a row.
4. **Intros are two paragraphs, maximum.** Get into the topic immediately. No slow warm-up, no rhetorical question with an obvious answer.
5. **No dividers between sections.** Use headings and white space, not decorative rules.
6. **Bullets used sparingly.** Only for genuinely separate items, like a food list. If three points flow as a sentence, write them as one.
7. **Let style shift slightly between posts.** The voice stays consistent, the execution doesn't have to be identical every time.
8. **Never open or close two posts the same way.** Each one finds its own entry point.
9. **Don't over-polish.** A slightly rough sentence that sounds real beats one polished into blandness.
10. **Write with specificity.** Not "many women struggle with luteal fatigue." Instead: "if you've cancelled plans the week before your period more than once, this is for you."

## 5. Post Structure

**Word count:** 600 to 1,000 words per article (this can flex slightly for
recipe roundups or deep PCOS guides, but shouldn't balloon past 1,200).

**The five-angle cluster.** Most keyword clusters in the content sheet
repeat the same five angles per sub-topic. Match the structure to the angle:

| Angle | What it needs |
|---|---|
| Pillar post ("Follicular Phase Foods to...") | The core how-to. Practical and complete on its own. |
| "Ultimate Guide" | Same topic, more depth. More sections, more specifics. |
| "Common Mistakes" | Troubleshooting framing. Name what goes wrong and why. |
| "For Busy Schedules" | Strip it down. Fewer steps, faster wins. |
| "Science-Backed" | Lead with the mechanism (the hormone, the compound), then the application. |

**Format types:**
- **The Guide:** practical sections, no numbered list, walks through reasoning and application
- **The Listicle:** used mainly for recipe roundups and food lists, H3 per item, short and specific
- **The Troubleshooting Post:** names a mistake, explains why it backfires, gives the fix

## 6. In-Article Promotion

This is what makes each article part of the funnel, not just a standalone
post. See the project brief for the full funnel logic; the rules for the
article itself:

- Every post includes exactly one `<NewsletterInline />` component (a
  direct link to the free guide's Gumroad page), placed after the reader
  has gotten real value, usually after the first or second content
  section. Never above the fold.
- Every post ends with the `HandbookCTA`, which pulls its label from the
  post's `ctaLabel` frontmatter field. Write that label as a specific,
  benefit-led sentence, not "check out my ebook."
- No more than one contextual mid-body mention of the Handbook beyond the
  closing CTA. The funnel works on volume of articles, not pressure per
  article.

## 7. SEO Rules

- The focus keyword (the post's `keyword` frontmatter field) appears
  naturally in the first paragraph and two to four more times through the
  piece. Never forced.
- The `excerpt` frontmatter field doubles as the meta description: 17 to
  20 words, includes the keyword, reads like a real sentence, not a
  keyword string.
- Headings (`##`, `###`) should be descriptive and specific. Skip keyword
  stuffing in headings.
- The post title comes directly from the content sheet. Don't rewrite it,
  it's already built to work as a Pinterest pin headline.

## 8. Writing a New Post: The Mechanics

New posts live in `content/posts/` as `.mdx` files. Frontmatter fields:

```yaml
---
title: "Exact title from the content sheet"
category: "follicular-phase"   # must match a slug in lib/categories.ts
keyword: "follicular phase diet"
painPoint: "Low energy after your period, hormone rebuilding"
excerpt: "17-20 word meta description, written as a real sentence."
date: "2026-09-10"
ctaLabel: "One sentence pointing to the specific Handbook chapter or bonus this row's CTA angle names."
---
```

Drop `<NewsletterInline />` on its own line where the free-guide link
should appear. The `HandbookCTA` at the end is automatic, it reads
`ctaLabel` from frontmatter, no need to add it manually.

## 9. Quality Checklist

Before any post is published:

- [ ] No em dashes anywhere in the post
- [ ] No sentence longer than 25 words
- [ ] Intro is two paragraphs maximum
- [ ] No template phrasing
- [ ] No hollow superlatives without a real number or reason behind them
- [ ] At least one real opinion or point of view
- [ ] Specificity used in place of generality at least three times
- [ ] `<NewsletterInline />` (free-guide link) placed once, after real value has been given
- [ ] `ctaLabel` frontmatter matches this row's CTA angle from the content sheet
- [ ] Focus keyword appears naturally in the first paragraph
- [ ] Excerpt is 17 to 20 words and reads like a sentence
- [ ] The post sounds like a real woman who did the research, not a content machine

## The One Overriding Principle

Every post should sound like a friend who actually figured out her cycle,
not like a content machine filling a keyword cell in a spreadsheet.

If a sentence sounds templated, rewrite it. If a section reads like every
other phase-diet post on Pinterest, change the angle. The reader can tell
the difference. Write for her.
