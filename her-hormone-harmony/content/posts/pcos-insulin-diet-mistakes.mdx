You cut the soda. You started pairing carbs with protein most of the time, most days. And the belly fat and cravings barely budged, which feels like proof that none of this actually works for your body specifically, even though it's supposedly the right approach.

More likely, one or two details are quietly undoing the rest of the effort without you realizing it. Here's where it usually goes wrong, starting with the most common.

## Mistake one: going low-carb instead of low-glycemic

Cutting carbs dramatically often backfires with PCOS specifically, since very low carb intake can raise cortisol as your body adapts to the restriction, and elevated cortisol worsens insulin resistance right back, partially canceling out the intended benefit. The goal is choosing slower-digesting carbs and pairing them well, not eliminating them from your plate entirely. A diet you can't sustain for six months isn't really solving a problem that took years to develop, it's just delaying the same result.

## Mistake two: ignoring fruit juice and "healthy" smoothies

A large fruit smoothie, even one packed with spinach and a scoop of protein powder, can spike blood sugar nearly as hard as a can of soda if it's mostly fruit and lacks enough fat or fiber to slow the absorption down. Whole fruit with the fiber intact behaves very differently in your body than the same fruit blended into liquid, since blending breaks down the plant structure that would otherwise slow digestion. This is one of the most common blind spots, because smoothies feel virtuous even when the blood sugar impact tells a different story.

## Mistake three: skipping meals to "save calories"

Skipping meals, especially breakfast, often leads to a bigger blood sugar spike at the next meal, since you're arriving hungrier, eating faster, and frequently choosing something quick and refined out of urgency. It also increases cortisol over the course of the day, which independently worsens insulin resistance on top of whatever you eventually eat. Three balanced meals usually outperforms two meals and a long gap between them, even when the total calories across the day end up roughly the same.

## Mistake four: doing cardio and calling it enough

Steady-state cardio burns calories in the moment but does less for long-term insulin sensitivity than strength training does, because muscle tissue is what actually improves how your body handles glucose at rest, hours after the workout ends. Cardio alone doesn't build much of that tissue, which means it can feel like you're doing plenty of exercise without addressing the specific mechanism this whole diet approach is targeting. This doesn't mean stop your cardio if you enjoy it, it means don't skip strength work under the assumption that cardio already has it covered.

## Mistake five: expecting the scale to move before anything else does

Insulin resistance often improves in ways that don't show up on a scale immediately: steadier energy through the afternoon, fewer intense cravings, less of that specific crash-and-hunger cycle a few hours after eating. Weight loss frequently follows these changes rather than leading them, sometimes by a matter of months. Judging progress by the scale alone in the first four to six weeks means missing the earlier signs that things are actually working underneath the surface.

## A pattern that compounds all of the above: inconsistent weekends

Many women follow this closely Monday through Friday and let weekends run on a completely different set of rules, larger portions, more alcohol, skipped structure entirely. Two days of undoing five days of careful blood sugar management meaningfully slows overall progress, not because weekends need to be identical to weekdays, but because the gap often ends up larger than intended without a plan for it.

## The fix, in one sentence

Match the effort to the actual mechanism: steady carbs paired with protein, movement that builds muscle, consistent meal timing across the whole week including weekends, and patience measured in months, not days.
