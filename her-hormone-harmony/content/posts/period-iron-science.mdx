Iron deficiency related to menstrual blood loss is one of the more well-documented nutritional issues specific to women's health, backed by decades of research rather than a newer or less established claim.

## How common is menstrual iron deficiency, according to research

Studies estimate iron deficiency affects a substantial portion of menstruating women globally, with heavier menstrual bleeding identified as one of the leading causes in populations with otherwise adequate dietary iron access. Research specifically measuring iron status against self-reported flow heaviness shows a clear, consistent relationship between heavier bleeding and lower iron stores.

## What research says about heme versus non-heme absorption

Studies measuring iron absorption rates confirm heme iron is absorbed considerably more efficiently, in the range of 15 to 35 percent, compared to non-heme iron's more variable 2 to 20 percent, heavily dependent on what else is consumed in the same meal. This research directly supports the practical emphasis on pairing non-heme sources with absorption enhancers rather than assuming all iron sources are functionally equivalent.

## The vitamin C research, specifically

Controlled studies adding vitamin C to meals containing non-heme iron show measurable increases in absorption, in some studies multiplying absorption several times over compared to the same meal without vitamin C present. This is one of the more robust, consistently replicated findings in iron absorption research, giving strong support to this specific pairing strategy.

## What research says about calcium's inhibitory effect

Studies on calcium and iron co-consumption consistently show reduced iron absorption when both are present in the same meal, an effect observed with both dietary calcium and calcium supplements. This research supports the practical recommendation to separate calcium and iron intake by at least an hour or two, rather than being an overly cautious suggestion without real basis.

## Research on symptoms beyond fatigue

Studies on iron deficiency symptoms document a range beyond fatigue alone, including pica, unusual cravings for non-food substances like ice, restless leg syndrome, and cognitive symptoms like difficulty concentrating. This research supports paying attention to this broader symptom cluster, not just fatigue in isolation, when considering whether iron deficiency might be relevant.

## What research says about ferritin as the most useful test

Studies comparing different iron status markers show ferritin, which reflects total body iron stores, as a more sensitive early indicator of developing deficiency than a standard complete blood count alone, which may not show abnormalities until deficiency has progressed further. This supports specifically requesting a ferritin test rather than assuming a normal general blood count rules out an iron issue.

## Where the research leaves genuine uncertainty

Individual variation in how much iron loss any specific period causes, and how much dietary intake is needed to compensate, varies considerably based on flow volume, existing stores, and individual absorption efficiency. This supports getting personally tested rather than assuming a generic recommendation applies precisely to your specific situation.

## What research says about iron and exercise performance

Studies on iron status and exercise capacity show that even mild iron deficiency, before it progresses to full anemia, can measurably reduce endurance and increase perceived exertion during physical activity. This is relevant for anyone training consistently while also managing heavier periods, since compromised iron status may explain reduced performance that's mistakenly attributed entirely to training factors.

## What research says about how quickly stores can be rebuilt

Studies tracking ferritin recovery during supplementation show meaningful increases typically within four to eight weeks of consistent, appropriately dosed supplementation, though full normalization for significant deficiency can take several months. This gives a research-grounded timeline for what "working" actually looks like, rather than an arbitrary guess.

## Applying the research honestly

The strongest evidence supports menstrual blood loss as a genuine, well-documented cause of iron deficiency in a meaningful portion of women, with heme iron, vitamin C pairing, and avoiding calcium and tannin interference as directly research-supported practical strategies. This is a well-established area of nutritional research, not a claim resting on thin or anecdotal evidence.
