You've cut back on salt, maybe added more water, and the bloating around ovulation still shows up right on schedule. Before assuming nothing actually helps, check whether one of these patterns is working against the effort.

## Mistake one: drinking less water because you're already retaining fluid

It feels logical to cut back on water when you're already bloated, but under-hydration can trigger your body to retain even more fluid as a protective response, working directly against the goal. Continuing consistent water intake, rather than restricting it, supports proper fluid regulation instead of undermining it.

## Mistake two: drastically increasing fiber right when bloating starts

Adding a large amount of fiber specifically during the days you're already bloated can temporarily worsen symptoms, since a sudden increase requires adjustment time your gut doesn't have during this brief window. Fiber helps most when intake is consistent across your whole cycle, not spiked reactively once bloating has already started.

## Mistake three: not addressing sodium until bloating has already started

Cutting salt only after noticing bloating means the fluid retention process is already underway by the time you make the change. Reducing high-sodium foods in the two to three days leading up to expected ovulation, based on your typical cycle length, works more effectively as prevention than as a reaction.

## Mistake four: assuming all bloating during your cycle is the same

Treating ovulation bloating and pre-period bloating identically misses that they're driven by different hormonal mechanisms, estrogen and fluid balance around ovulation, progesterone and prostaglandins before your period, which means the same approach doesn't necessarily address both equally well. Identifying which window you're actually in helps you apply the more relevant strategy.

## Mistake five: skipping movement because bloating feels uncomfortable

The instinct to stay still when bloated is understandable, but a short walk actually supports digestive motility mechanically, helping move things along rather than making discomfort worse. Gentle movement tends to ease this specific type of bloating rather than aggravate it.

## A sixth pattern: not distinguishing hormonal bloating from a food sensitivity

If bloating shows up at other points in your cycle too, not just around ovulation, a specific food sensitivity or digestive issue unrelated to your cycle might be contributing alongside the hormonal pattern. Tracking bloating against your cycle for a couple of months clarifies whether it's genuinely tied to ovulation or happening for a separate reason that deserves its own attention.

## A seventh pattern: wearing restrictive clothing through the discomfort

Tight waistbands or restrictive clothing during a window your body is already dealing with fluid retention and slower digestion can add unnecessary physical discomfort on top of the underlying bloating. Choosing looser, more comfortable clothing for the day or two this typically lasts isn't a fix, but it removes an easily avoidable source of added discomfort.

## An eighth pattern: giving up on tracking after one confusing cycle

A single cycle with an unusual stressor, travel, or illness can make the bloating pattern look inconsistent or unrelated to ovulation timing, when really an outside factor was compounding or masking the usual pattern that month. Tracking across two or three cycles gives a clearer, fairer picture than judging from one atypical month.

## What actually works

Maintain consistent hydration rather than restricting it, keep fiber intake steady across your whole cycle rather than spiking it reactively, reduce sodium preventively before expected ovulation, recognize this as distinct from pre-period bloating, and keep moving gently rather than staying still through the discomfort.
