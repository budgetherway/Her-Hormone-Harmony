Soaking through a pad or tampon in under two hours, passing clots larger than a quarter, or bleeding for more than seven days isn't just an inconvenient period, it's a pattern with a name, menorrhagia, and real physiological causes behind it worth understanding before assuming it's simply how your body works.

## What actually causes heavy periods

An imbalance between estrogen and progesterone, particularly excess estrogen relative to progesterone, causes the uterine lining to build up thicker than usual, resulting in heavier bleeding when it sheds. Other causes include fibroids, benign growths in the uterus, and conditions like polyps, both of which can significantly increase bleeding volume independent of hormone levels.

## Why this matters beyond the inconvenience

Heavy bleeding repeated month after month can lead to iron deficiency significant enough to cause real fatigue, dizziness, and difficulty concentrating, since blood loss this substantial often outpaces what diet alone can replace. This connects directly to the iron-related fatigue many women experience after a heavy period, making heavy flow itself worth addressing, not just the resulting tiredness.

## Food that supports a more balanced flow

Foods that support healthy estrogen metabolism, cruciferous vegetables, fiber, fermented foods, can help address excess estrogen relative to progesterone, one of the more common underlying contributors to heavy bleeding. Anti-inflammatory foods, particularly omega-3s, may help modulate the prostaglandin activity involved in both bleeding volume and cramping intensity.

## What tends to make heavy bleeding worse

High-sugar, highly processed food contributes to inflammation and can worsen the hormonal imbalance underlying heavy bleeding for some women, working against efforts to address it nutritionally. Excess alcohol also affects estrogen metabolism in the liver, potentially compounding an existing imbalance.

## The role of stress and thyroid function in this picture

Chronic stress can disrupt the hormonal signaling involved in regulating your cycle, and in some cases contributes to heavier or more irregular bleeding as part of a broader hormonal disruption. Thyroid dysfunction, both underactive and overactive, is a well-documented cause of changes in menstrual flow, including heavier bleeding, making thyroid function worth ruling out if heavy periods appeared or worsened without an obvious explanation.

## Specific foods worth prioritizing, beyond the general categories

Ginger has some evidence for reducing menstrual blood loss when taken consistently during the days of bleeding, alongside its more established role in reducing cramping. Vitamin K, found in leafy greens, plays a role in blood clotting and is worth ensuring adequate intake of, since it supports the body's normal clotting response during bleeding.

## Iron becomes non-negotiable with this pattern

Since heavy bleeding causes more significant iron loss than a typical period, prioritizing iron-rich food, or supplementation if a doctor recommends it, matters more here than for someone with a lighter, more typical flow. This isn't optional support, it's a direct response to a larger deficit than average.

## What a realistic timeline looks like

If hormonal imbalance is contributing to heavy bleeding, dietary changes supporting estrogen metabolism typically take two to three months of consistent effort before any change in flow becomes noticeable, since this reflects a gradual hormonal shift rather than an immediate fix. This isn't a pattern that responds to a single cycle of changed eating.

## Tracking your flow to have useful information for a doctor

Noting roughly how many pads or tampons you use per day, whether you're passing clots and how large, and how many days bleeding lasts gives concrete information that's genuinely useful if you do end up discussing this with a doctor, rather than relying on a vague sense that "it feels heavy" when asked directly.

## When food changes aren't enough, and why that's important to know

Heavy periods caused by fibroids, polyps, or certain bleeding disorders won't respond to dietary changes alone, regardless of how consistently they're followed, since the underlying cause isn't primarily hormonal. This is important to know upfront rather than spending months on food changes for a cause that food can't address.

## When to see a doctor, without delay

Soaking through protection every hour or two for several consecutive hours, passing clots larger than a quarter, bleeding longer than seven days, or feeling severely fatigued or dizzy all warrant a doctor's visit rather than continued self-management. Heavy bleeding this significant deserves a proper evaluation to rule out fibroids, polyps, thyroid issues, or bleeding disorders, all of which have specific treatments beyond food and lifestyle support.
