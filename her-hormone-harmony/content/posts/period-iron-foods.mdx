If the exhaustion and occasional dizziness after your period feels heavier than simple tiredness, there's a real, measurable reason. Menstrual blood loss removes iron from your body, and for a lot of women, especially those with heavier flows, that loss outpaces what a typical diet replaces, leaving a genuine deficit behind long after bleeding stops.

## Why iron loss specifically causes this exhaustion

Iron is a core component of hemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells responsible for carrying oxygen throughout your body. When iron stores drop, your blood carries less oxygen efficiently, which shows up as fatigue, difficulty concentrating, and in more significant cases, dizziness or shortness of breath with normal activity. This is a mechanical, measurable process, not simply feeling run down after a hard week.

## Who is most affected by this

Women with heavier periods, defined as needing to change a pad or tampon every hour or two for several hours, or periods lasting longer than seven days, lose meaningfully more iron each cycle than women with lighter flow. Over months and years, this repeated loss can outpace replacement even with a reasonably good diet, gradually depleting stored iron before it shows up as a diagnosable deficiency.

## The best food sources, and why type matters

Heme iron, found in red meat, poultry, and fish, is absorbed considerably more efficiently by the body than non-heme iron, found in plant sources like spinach and lentils. This doesn't mean plant sources aren't useful, it means non-heme iron benefits significantly from being paired with vitamin C, which enhances its absorption meaningfully.

Red meat, particularly beef and lamb, oysters, and dark poultry meat are among the most concentrated heme iron sources. Lentils, spinach, tofu, and fortified cereals provide meaningful non-heme iron, especially useful for anyone eating a largely plant-based diet.

## Pairing for better absorption

A vitamin C source, citrus, bell peppers, strawberries, eaten alongside a non-heme iron source can significantly increase how much iron your body actually absorbs from that meal. Spinach with lemon dressing, or lentils with a side of bell pepper, applies this pairing without requiring separate supplements.

## What blocks iron absorption

Calcium, tea, and coffee all interfere with iron absorption when consumed at the same meal, which is worth knowing if you're intentionally trying to boost intake during and right after your period specifically. This doesn't mean eliminating any of these, it means spacing them an hour or two away from your main iron-focused meals rather than consuming them together.

## Cooking methods that affect iron content

Cooking in a cast iron skillet can meaningfully increase the iron content of food, particularly acidic dishes like tomato sauce, which leach more iron from the pan during cooking. This is a low-effort, essentially free way to boost iron intake without changing what you're actually eating, just what you're cooking it in.

## Symptoms worth paying attention to beyond fatigue

Iron deficiency can also show up as unusual cravings for ice, known as pica, brittle nails, hair thinning, or a pale complexion, symptoms that are easy to attribute to other causes without recognizing the underlying connection to iron status. Noticing a cluster of these alongside post-period exhaustion strengthens the case for getting levels checked directly.

## What a realistic day of eating might look like

Breakfast: fortified cereal or oatmeal with strawberries. Lunch: a lentil salad with bell peppers and a citrus vinaigrette. Dinner: lean beef or chicken with spinach sautéed in olive oil. Spacing coffee or tea to a different time than these meals supports absorption rather than working against it.

## When food alone isn't enough

If exhaustion and dizziness are significant, or persist well beyond your period into the following weeks, it's worth asking a doctor for a ferritin test, which measures your body's stored iron more accurately than a standard blood count alone. Iron deficiency significant enough to require supplementation is common and very treatable, and food changes alone may not be sufficient if stores have dropped substantially.

## Building this into a recurring habit

Since this pattern repeats every cycle, particularly for women with heavier periods, treating iron-focused eating as a recurring habit during and right after your period, rather than something to remember only when symptoms feel severe, helps prevent stores from gradually depleting over months of repeated loss.
