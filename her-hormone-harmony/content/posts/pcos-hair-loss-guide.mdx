If you've already tried the basics, spearmint tea, cutting sugar, and you're still watching more hair collect in the drain than feels normal, you need the fuller picture, not the beginner version. PCOS hair loss is a chronic condition with a chronic cause, and it usually needs more than one lever pulled at once to actually shift.

This is the complete list of what matters, roughly ranked by how much evidence and impact each one carries, so you can prioritize instead of trying to do fifteen things at once.

## The core mechanism, one more time

Everything below exists to interrupt one process: excess androgens converting into DHT via the 5-alpha reductase enzyme, then DHT shrinking your scalp follicles while enlarging follicles elsewhere on your face and body. Every intervention here either lowers androgen production, blocks that conversion, or reduces the insulin resistance that drives androgen production up in the first place. If a remedy doesn't touch one of those three points, it's probably not going to move the needle much, no matter how popular it is online.

## Food levers, ranked by evidence

**Spearmint tea.** Two cups daily, brewed for at least five minutes to actually extract the active compounds. Studies specifically on women with PCOS-related hirsutism showed measurable drops in free testosterone over a month of consistent use. This is one of the few remedies here with a real controlled trial behind it, which is why it tops the list despite being so simple.

**Zinc-rich foods.** Pumpkin seeds, chickpeas, lentils, and oysters if you eat them. Zinc inhibits the enzyme that converts testosterone into DHT, which is the exact mechanism causing follicle damage in the first place. Aim for zinc showing up in at least one meal most days rather than treating it as an occasional snack.

**Omega-3s.** Fatty fish, walnuts, and flaxseed reduce the inflammation that worsens insulin resistance, and insulin resistance sits upstream of androgen production. This one works indirectly, but the research on omega-3s and inflammatory markers in PCOS specifically is fairly solid.

**Low-glycemic eating overall.** Not a specific food so much as a pattern: pairing carbs with protein and fat, choosing whole grains over refined ones, and not skipping meals in a way that causes blood sugar crashes later. This pattern matters as much as any single food on this list, since it addresses the insulin piece that keeps androgen production elevated day after day.

## Supplement options worth a real conversation with a doctor

Saw palmetto has some evidence for blocking DHT production, similar to how it's used for male pattern hair loss, though PCOS-specific research is thinner than the spearmint data. Inositol, the same supplement often recommended for PCOS ovulation, also improves insulin sensitivity enough to indirectly help androgen levels, and it has one of the stronger overall evidence bases among PCOS supplements. DIM, derived from cruciferous vegetables, is sometimes recommended for hormone metabolism, though its evidence specifically for androgen-driven hair loss is more anecdotal than proven. None of these replace a doctor's input, especially if you're on other medications like birth control or metformin.

## Movement that actually helps

Strength training two to three times a week improves insulin sensitivity more reliably than cardio alone, and better insulin sensitivity means less androgen overproduction. This isn't about training harder or longer, it's about training in a way that specifically targets the mechanism at play. Even two twenty-minute sessions weekly, done consistently over months, outperform an ambitious routine abandoned after three weeks.

## Topical options, and their real limits

Minoxidil, sold as Rogaine, can slow scalp hair loss regardless of the underlying cause, including PCOS, by keeping follicles in their growth phase longer. It doesn't address the androgen problem underneath, which means facial hair growth continues unless you're also working the food and lifestyle side. Think of it as buying time on the scalp while you address the root cause elsewhere, not a standalone solution.

Anti-androgen topicals containing ingredients like spironolactone are sometimes prescribed off-label for scalp application, though this requires a dermatologist's involvement and isn't something to start on your own.

## Tracking progress without losing your mind

Take a photo of your part line once a month, same lighting, same angle, same time of day if possible. Weekly photos show nothing meaningful, since hair changes too slowly for week-to-week comparison to be useful, and it mostly just creates anxiety. Monthly comparisons over four to six months are what actually reveal whether something is working, and having photos also protects you from the memory bias that makes slow improvement invisible in the moment.

## What doesn't help, even though it's everywhere online

Biotin gets recommended constantly for hair loss, and unless you have an actual biotin deficiency, which is rare, it does very little for androgen-driven thinning specifically. Expensive "hormone balancing" hair vitamins with a dozen ingredients are usually biotin and a basic multivitamin with a significant markup attached. Hair growth "serums" with no androgen-related ingredients tend to work primarily on scalp health and circulation, which can help at the margins but won't touch the actual DHT problem underneath. Spend that money on pumpkin seeds and spearmint tea instead, and save the serum budget for something with mechanism-level evidence.

## The realistic outcome

This won't reverse hair loss overnight, and for some women with more severe PCOS, it slows progression more than it reverses it completely. That's still a meaningfully different outcome than doing nothing, and it's the honest version of what food and lifestyle changes can accomplish here, without promising something that isn't backed by how hair biology actually works.
