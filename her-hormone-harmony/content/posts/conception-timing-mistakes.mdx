You've tried timing intercourse around what you believed was your fertile window for several cycles without success, which is genuinely disheartening when you're following what seems like reasonable, widely repeated advice. Here's where that advice usually goes wrong, and why the results don't match the effort.

## Mistake one: assuming day 14 applies to your cycle

Day 14 assumes a perfect 28-day cycle with ovulation exactly at the midpoint, a pattern that describes a minority of women exactly. Ovulation timing is calculated backward from your next period, roughly fourteen days before it starts, not forward from your last one, which means anyone with a cycle longer or shorter than 28 days is working from an inaccurate assumption every single month without realizing it.

## Mistake two: targeting only the day of ovulation

Because sperm survive up to five days while the egg survives less than twenty-four hours, the days leading up to ovulation matter as much as the day itself, sometimes more, since sperm need time to be present and waiting when the egg releases. Waiting for confirmed ovulation, then having intercourse, often means timing has already passed the highest-probability window.

## Mistake three: relying on temperature alone for timing, not just confirmation

Basal body temperature confirms ovulation happened after the fact, typically one to two days later, which makes it useful for building a longer-term pattern but poorly suited for timing intercourse for that specific cycle. Using cervical mucus or OPKs, which provide advance notice, for actual timing decisions, while using temperature to confirm the pattern afterward, uses each method for what it's actually good at.

## Mistake four: skipping fertile-window days due to timing app confusion

Apps that predict ovulation from calendar averages alone can be off by several days for anyone with cycle variation, and following that prediction rather than your own tracked cervical mucus or OPK results means potentially missing the real window while confidently targeting the wrong days. Real, current-cycle data should always take priority over an app's calendar-based estimate when the two disagree.

## Mistake five: adding pressure that makes the process harder to sustain

Treating every fertile window as a high-stakes, must-succeed event adds psychological pressure that can make the whole process feel exhausting within a few months, sometimes affecting the relationship dynamics around intercourse itself. Approaching the fertile window as "we'll aim for every day or two this week" rather than a single make-or-break day tends to be more sustainable over the months this process can genuinely take.

## A sixth mistake: not addressing sperm health alongside timing

Focusing entirely on tracking the cycle while ignoring factors that affect sperm quality, frequent hot tub use, certain medications, high stress, significant alcohol intake, means only half the equation is being optimized. Both partners' health factors matter for the timing effort to have its best chance of working.

## A seventh mistake: judging results too early

Roughly 85 percent of couples with no underlying fertility issues conceive within a year of consistent, correctly timed attempts, and a meaningful portion of that group takes more than three or four cycles even with accurate timing. Judging the approach as failed after one or two cycles doesn't reflect the normal range of how long conception actually takes for most couples.

## An eighth mistake: not tracking which cycles were actually well-timed

Without noting which cycles involved accurate tracking and well-timed intercourse versus which were more uncertain or rushed, it's hard to fairly evaluate whether the approach itself is working. A cycle where tracking was inconsistent or travel disrupted timing isn't a fair test of the method, and treating it as one skews your sense of whether adjustments are actually needed.

## A ninth mistake: letting the process create tension between partners

Turning the fertile window into a scheduled obligation rather than something both partners feel involved in can create friction that makes an already emotionally loaded process harder to sustain over several months. Approaching timing as a shared effort, with both partners aware of the general window rather than one partner managing it alone, tends to hold up better over time.

## What actually improves the odds

Track your specific cycle's cervical mucus and OPK results rather than assuming a calendar day, aim for intercourse every one to two days across the fertile window rather than a single target day, use temperature for pattern-building rather than real-time timing, address sperm health factors for both partners, and give the approach several cycles before concluding it isn't working.
