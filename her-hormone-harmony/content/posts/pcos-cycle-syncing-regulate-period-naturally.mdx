If you haven't had a period in months, most cycle syncing advice doesn't apply to you yet, and that's frustrating to hear when everyone's talking about tracking phases you don't currently have. PCOS often means irregular or absent ovulation, which means the whole four-phase framework needs a different starting point.

Here's the honest version: you can't sync with a cycle that isn't happening. The real first step is making ovulation more likely at all, and food plays a bigger role in that than most PCOS advice admits.

## Why PCOS disrupts the cycle in the first place

Insulin resistance shows up in most PCOS cases, even in women who aren't overweight. High insulin pushes your ovaries to produce more androgens, and those elevated androgens are what block regular ovulation. Fix the insulin picture, and ovulation often becomes possible again on its own.

## What actually moves the needle

Lower the glycemic load of what you eat without cutting carbs out entirely. Pair carbohydrates with protein and fat every time: fruit with nut butter instead of fruit alone, rice with chicken and vegetables instead of rice by itself. This blunts the insulin spike that drives androgen production.

Fiber matters more here than almost anywhere else. Twenty-five to thirty grams a day from vegetables, legumes, and whole grains helps your body clear excess androgens through digestion instead of recirculating them.

Inositol, specifically a 40:1 ratio of myo-inositol to D-chiro-inositol, has real research behind it for improving insulin sensitivity and restoring ovulation in PCOS. It's worth asking a doctor about directly rather than guessing at a random supplement online.

## Movement, but not the way you think

Long, hard cardio sessions can actually raise cortisol enough to worsen the insulin resistance already driving your PCOS. Strength training two to three times a week does more for insulin sensitivity than an hour on the treadmill, and it's easier to sustain besides.

## The timeline to expect

This isn't a one-cycle fix. Most women see meaningful changes over three to six months of consistent changes, not three weeks. That's a hard thing to hear when you just want your period back, but rushing it with extreme diets tends to backfire and stall progress further.

None of this replaces a proper workup. If your period has been gone for more than three months, ask your doctor to check your insulin, thyroid, and androgen levels before spending a year guessing at food changes alone.
