If a general effort to eat more iron-rich food hasn't resolved persistent post-period exhaustion, the details of type, timing, and absorption matter as much as simply eating more of it. Here's the complete picture.

## Heme versus non-heme iron, in more depth

Heme iron, from animal sources, is absorbed at roughly 15 to 35 percent efficiency, while non-heme iron, from plant sources, is absorbed at a considerably lower 2 to 20 percent, heavily influenced by what else is eaten in the same meal. This difference means a vegetarian or vegan diet can still provide adequate iron, but it requires more deliberate attention to both quantity and absorption-boosting pairings than a diet that includes heme sources.

## The complete list of absorption enhancers

Vitamin C is the most well-established enhancer, capable of significantly increasing non-heme iron absorption when eaten in the same meal. Vitamin A and beta-carotene, found in orange and yellow vegetables, also support absorption to a lesser degree. Fermented foods may modestly help by reducing compounds in plant foods that otherwise inhibit iron absorption.

## The complete list of absorption inhibitors

Calcium, whether from dairy or supplements, competes with iron for absorption when consumed together. Tannins in tea and coffee bind to iron and reduce its availability. Phytates in whole grains and legumes also inhibit absorption somewhat, though soaking, sprouting, or fermenting these foods reduces phytate content meaningfully.

## Building a full day around this goal

Space calcium-rich foods and beverages like coffee or tea at least one to two hours away from your main iron-focused meals. Pair every non-heme iron source with a vitamin C food in the same sitting. If eating heme sources, timing matters less since heme iron absorption isn't affected by these inhibitors and enhancers the same way non-heme iron is.

## When supplementation becomes necessary

If dietary changes alone aren't resolving symptoms after a few months, or if a ferritin test confirms significantly depleted stores, iron supplementation becomes a reasonable next step, ideally guided by a doctor who can recommend an appropriate dose and form based on your specific test results. Iron supplements taken with vitamin C absorb better, while taking them with calcium or dairy reduces their effectiveness the same way food-based calcium does.

## Managing supplement side effects

Iron supplements commonly cause constipation and stomach upset, which can be reduced by taking a lower dose more frequently rather than one large dose, or by choosing a gentler form like iron bisglycinate over ferrous sulfate. Taking supplements with food reduces stomach upset, though it also somewhat reduces absorption, a tradeoff worth discussing with whoever recommended the supplement.

## What to do about persistent constipation from supplementation

Adequate fiber and water intake alongside iron supplementation helps offset the constipating effect many women experience, and magnesium citrate, if already being used for other reasons, can also help counterbalance this specific side effect. Switching iron forms rather than stopping supplementation entirely is usually the better solution if constipation becomes genuinely uncomfortable.

## How pregnancy and breastfeeding change iron needs

Iron requirements increase substantially during pregnancy, and while this is a different context than typical menstrual iron loss, it's worth knowing that any existing iron deficiency going into pregnancy compounds with these increased needs. Addressing iron status before trying to conceive, if that's part of your planning, gives you a stronger starting point.

## What a realistic timeline looks like

Dietary changes alone typically take one to three months to meaningfully improve mild iron deficiency, while supplementation for more significant deficiency often takes two to three months to normalize levels, with symptom improvement sometimes noticeable before lab values fully normalize. This is a gradual process, not an overnight fix, regardless of which approach you're using.

## Retesting and monitoring progress

If you've started supplementing or made significant dietary changes specifically to address confirmed iron deficiency, retesting ferritin after two to three months gives a concrete way to confirm progress rather than relying on symptoms alone, which can be influenced by many factors beyond iron status specifically.
