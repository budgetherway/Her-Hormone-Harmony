You've added spinach, maybe some lentils, and the post-period exhaustion hasn't budged. Before assuming iron isn't your issue after all, check whether one of these patterns is undermining the effort.

## Mistake one: eating iron-rich food alongside coffee or tea

Having a cup of coffee or tea with your iron-focused meal significantly reduces how much iron your body actually absorbs from that meal, regardless of how iron-rich the food itself is. Spacing these beverages at least an hour away from meals you're specifically eating for iron protects the absorption you're trying to achieve.

## Mistake two: relying entirely on non-heme sources without pairing them well

Spinach and lentils provide real iron, but non-heme iron absorbs poorly on its own, sometimes below 5 percent, without a vitamin C source eaten in the same meal. Eating these foods without any pairing strategy means you may be consuming meaningful iron on paper while absorbing very little of it in practice.

## Mistake three: taking a calcium supplement or drinking milk with iron-focused meals

Calcium directly competes with iron for absorption, meaning a glass of milk or a calcium supplement taken alongside an iron-rich meal can significantly blunt how much iron gets absorbed. This is easy to miss since calcium and iron aren't typically thought of as related, but the competition between them is well established.

## Mistake four: not addressing an underlying heavy period

If your period is genuinely heavy, extended dietary iron alone may not keep pace with ongoing loss each cycle, meaning food changes help but don't fully resolve the deficit without also addressing why blood loss is heavier than typical in the first place. This is worth discussing with a doctor rather than only intensifying food-based efforts indefinitely.

## Mistake five: expecting food changes to work as fast as a supplement would

Dietary iron increases stores more gradually than supplementation, often taking a couple of months of consistent effort to show a meaningful difference in symptoms. Judging food-based changes as ineffective after a week or two doesn't reflect how gradually this process actually works compared to a more concentrated supplement dose.

## A sixth pattern: not getting an actual ferritin test before assuming the cause

Fatigue has many possible causes, and assuming iron deficiency without confirming it through a ferritin test means potentially missing a different underlying issue, a thyroid problem, sleep disorder, or something else entirely, while directing all your effort toward iron alone. A simple blood test removes the guesswork.

## A seventh pattern: cooking iron-rich food in ways that reduce its content

Overcooking vegetables like spinach can reduce their overall nutrient content, including iron, compared to lightly cooking or eating them raw when appropriate. This is a smaller factor than the pairing and timing mistakes above, but it's worth knowing if you're already doing everything else right and still not seeing the expected result.

## An eighth pattern: stopping supplementation as soon as symptoms improve slightly

Feeling somewhat better after a few weeks of supplementation doesn't necessarily mean iron stores have fully recovered, since symptoms can improve before ferritin levels normalize completely. Stopping too early risks a relapse once the partial improvement plateaus, rather than continuing until a follow-up test confirms stores are genuinely restored.

## What actually works

Space iron-rich meals away from coffee, tea, and calcium sources, pair non-heme iron with vitamin C consistently, address a genuinely heavy period directly rather than only compensating through diet, give food-based changes a couple of months before judging results, and confirm the actual cause with a ferritin test rather than assuming.
