A lot of conception advice narrows down to one piece of guidance: have sex on the day you ovulate. That framing misses most of what actually matters, since an egg only survives about twelve to twenty-four hours after release, while sperm can survive inside the body for up to five days. Your actual fertile window is considerably wider than a single day, and understanding why changes how you'd want to time things.

## Why the fertile window starts before ovulation, not on it

Because sperm can live in fertile cervical mucus for several days, intercourse in the days leading up to ovulation can still result in conception once the egg is released, even though the egg itself wasn't present yet at the time of intercourse. This means the days before ovulation matter as much as, arguably more than, the day of ovulation itself, since sperm are already waiting when the egg becomes available.

## The actual window, laid out

Research generally places the fertile window at about six days: the five days leading up to ovulation and the day of ovulation itself. Conception chances rise as you get closer to ovulation, peaking on the day of and the two days immediately before it, then dropping off sharply after ovulation since the egg's survival window is so short by comparison.

## Why timing based on ovulation confirmation alone is often too late

Basal body temperature confirms ovulation happened after the fact, typically one to two days later, which means waiting for a temperature spike before having intercourse often means missing the highest-probability days entirely. This is exactly why cervical mucus and OPKs, which give advance notice rather than after-the-fact confirmation, matter more for actual conception timing than temperature tracking alone.

## What this looks like in practice

Rather than aiming for a single "perfect day," intercourse every one to two days during the week leading up to and including your estimated ovulation day covers the full fertile window without requiring pinpoint precision on exactly which day the egg releases. This approach also removes the pressure of trying to hit one specific day exactly right, which can add unnecessary stress to an already emotionally loaded process.

## How irregular cycles change this planning

If your cycle length varies significantly month to month, relying on a fixed calendar day each cycle doesn't work, since your fertile window will fall on a different day relative to your period each time. Tracking cervical mucus changes that specific cycle, rather than counting days from a calendar, adjusts automatically to whatever that particular cycle is actually doing.

## Frequency questions worth addressing directly

More frequent intercourse than every other day doesn't meaningfully improve conception odds for most couples and can add pressure without added benefit, while abstaining for many days to "save up" sperm can actually reduce sperm quality in some cases. Every one to two days during the fertile window strikes a reasonable, evidence-supported balance without either extreme.

## What age and other factors change about this picture

Fertility naturally declines with age, more noticeably after the mid-thirties, which means the same tracking and timing principles apply but the margin for imprecise timing narrows somewhat as age increases. This isn't a reason to panic over one slightly mistimed cycle, but it is a reason to prioritize accurate tracking sooner rather than relying on trial and error over many months if age is a factor in your specific situation.

## Why this window feels different from what most people expect

Most casual conception advice implies a single perfect day exists, which sets up an expectation that doesn't match how sperm and egg survival actually work together. Understanding the six-day window as a real range, not a single moment to hit exactly, tends to reduce the anxiety that comes from feeling like you need to identify one precise day among thirty and get it exactly right.

## What to do if you're unsure exactly where you are in your cycle

If you haven't been tracking closely and aren't sure whether you're approaching, at, or past ovulation this cycle, checking cervical mucus today gives you a same-day read on roughly where you stand. Dry or minimal suggests you're likely before the fertile window, clear and stretchy suggests you're in it now, and a return to drier or thicker mucus suggests ovulation has likely passed for this cycle.

## When timing alone isn't the answer

If you've been tracking and timing intercourse accurately for six months without success under age 35, or three months over age 35, that's a reasonable point to involve a doctor or fertility specialist rather than continuing to adjust timing alone. Timing addresses one variable in conception, and ruling out other factors matters once careful timing hasn't produced results within a reasonable window.
